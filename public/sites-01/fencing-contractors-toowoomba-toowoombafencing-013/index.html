Boundary And Neighbour Checks

A dividing fence can be both a construction job and a neighbour process. The published guide refers to Queensland's Neighbourhood Disputes (Dividing Fences and Trees) Act 2011 for adjoining owners considering a sufficient dividing fence. It also notes that a premium material, extra height or preferred finish is not automatically shared.

Before comparing prices, establish the intended line and the standard being proposed. A written description and at least one quote give both owners something specific to review. If agreement is not reached, the guide says a notice to contribute is the formal step, with one month to reach agreement before a possible QCAT application within the stated period.

Height needs care where ground level, retaining walls or corner visibility are involved. The source page notes that combined height can change the approval question, and that a corner block can need a lower sight-line treatment so drivers can see across the intersection.

Read The Quote Inclusions

A metre rate can help with a rough check, but it cannot describe the whole site. Two prices are not comparable unless they treat posts, corners, gate hardware, existing fence conditions, access and sloping sections in the same way.

Rangeville and Mount Lofty are named as examples where slope can require stepped panels or a raked line. That does not make every property in those areas difficult, but it shows why the quote should respond to the actual run, including post positions, panel treatment and setting-out work.

Ask for the material, height, posts, gate approach and repair scope to be visible in writing. Where licensing is required, the guide says to check that the contractor holds a current QBCC licence with the right scope for the job.

Who This Applies To

This guide applies to Toowoomba property owners planning a boundary fence, gate, pool barrier, privacy screen, rural line, commercial edge or repair. It is most useful before a material has been chosen or when quotes appear to describe different work.

It also applies where a neighbour may be asked to contribute. Separate the shared boundary question from personal upgrades such as a preferred finish, extra height or added screening. That distinction helps the quote conversation stay tied to the proposed fence rather than assumptions about who should pay for every feature.

For repairs, ask whether leaning posts, broken rails or storm-damaged sections can be fixed, or whether replacement is more economical. That judgement should come from the condition of the run and the written repair scope.

Questions Before Booking

A useful enquiry gives enough site detail for a clearer response without pretending to be a construction plan. Start with what the fence must do, then add the constraints that could affect layout, compliance or price comparison.

  1. Which boundary or internal line is being fenced?
  2. Is the aim privacy, pool safety, rural containment, security, street presentation or repair?
  3. Does the run include slope, an old fence, tight access, a retaining wall or a corner block?
  4. Are gates needed, and should they be sliding, swing or automated?
  5. Is any part of the job tied to a neighbour discussion or contribution?

Those answers help each contractor respond to the same brief.

  1. Describe the run. Give the approximate length, location, old fence condition and any corners, returns or gates.
  2. Flag constraints. Mention slope, retaining walls, pool barriers, narrow access and neighbour involvement.
  3. Compare inclusions. Check posts, hardware, gate treatment, access assumptions and repair scope.
  4. Check licence scope. Where licensing is required, confirm the contractor holds a current QBCC licence with the right scope.
