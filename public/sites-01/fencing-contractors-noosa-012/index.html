Boundary and neighbour questions come before finishes

A dividing fence can involve both construction choices and a neighbour process. The source page refers to Queensland's Neighbourhood Disputes (Dividing Fences and Trees) Act 2011 and says adjoining owners considering a sufficient dividing fence should begin with a written description of the proposed work and at least one quote.

It also makes a careful distinction between a sufficient fence and upgraded preferences. Extra height, premium material or a preferred finish may not automatically be shared if one owner wants more than the sufficient outcome. That makes the scope document important: it should show the run, height, material, removal, gate allowances and any exclusions clearly enough for the neighbour conversation.

If agreement is not reached, the source page says a notice to contribute is the formal step. It notes one month to reach agreement after the notice, followed by a possible QCAT application within two months of the notice. This guide is general editorial information, not legal advice.

Material choice should follow the job the fence must do

The service categories on the source page are best read as a decision path rather than a menu. A privacy boundary, a street presentation fence, a pool barrier and a paddock boundary are solving different problems, so the same metre rate will not explain them properly.

For coastal residential work, the page points to Colorbond, aluminium slat fencing and screens, with attention to wind, salt air and front fence presentation. For hinterland and residential boundaries, it identifies paling, lapped and capped, and picket timber options. For rural work, it lists ringlock, star picket and strained wire fencing around acreage areas including Doonan, Verrierdale, Cooran and Kin Kin.

A pool barrier needs a separate check. The source page says pool fencing is built to the Queensland pool safety standard and that barrier requirements and inspection pathways are confirmed only by a licensed pool safety inspector. That makes a pool enquiry different from a standard boundary enquiry, even if the work appears close together on the same property.

How to read a fencing quote

A low metre figure can be misleading when the site is awkward. The source page says a rate can help with a rough project range, but it does not describe the whole site. It specifically calls out corners, returns, gates and hard to reach boundaries as items that can add time beyond straight metres.

Ground conditions also change the work. The page names coastal sand, riverfront alluvium and hinterland clay as different ground types that dig and hold posts differently. That is why a quote should state the footing assumptions rather than only listing the visible panel material.

  • Check whether removal and disposal of the old fence is included.
  • Ask how access limits, corners and gate openings have been allowed for.
  • Confirm the proposed height against ground levels and any retaining wall.
  • For a corner block, ask whether sight line treatment affects the front or side fence.
  • For pool areas, ask what must be checked by a licensed pool safety inspector.
