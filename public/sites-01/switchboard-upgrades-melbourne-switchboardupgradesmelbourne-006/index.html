Switchboard Upgrades Melbourne Explained

Switchboard Upgrades Melbourne describes older Melbourne homes with boards that have not been upgraded since the 1980s or earlier. These boards can use ceramic fuse type equipment, including rewireable fuses, without safety switches or with little spare capacity for modern household use.

Before requesting an assessment, note what the board is doing rather than trying to choose equipment from a photograph. Fuses that keep blowing, circuits that trip when the heater and kettle run together, and a board without RCD protection are useful details to report. They help the electrician understand the existing problem and decide what needs to be inspected.

  • Record whether the board uses ceramic fuse carriers.
  • Ask which circuits have safety switch protection.
  • List planned solar, smart meter or new appliance work.
  • Explain any repeated tripping or blown fuse issue.

The age of the board alone does not establish the correct replacement. The assessment needs to consider the board, wiring and circuits together.

Match protection to future demand

A modern board can replace old fuses with circuit breakers and safety switches, while providing room for future circuits. An RCD safety switch is designed to cut power when it detects current leaking to earth. An RCBO combines overload protection and safety switch protection in one device.

Ask the electrician to explain which protective device is proposed for each circuit. That makes the quotation easier to compare and clarifies whether the work is a straightforward equipment change or includes broader circuit work. The proposed arrangement should also account for the property's existing wiring and intended electrical load.

Most homes run on single phase power. Larger homes, workshops and properties with higher draw appliances may require three phase power, and the source guide describes this work as more involved. The assessing electrician should confirm which arrangement is appropriate rather than assuming that a larger board automatically needs three phase power.

If solar or a smart meter is planned, ask for compatibility to be considered in the same assessment. The guide specifically identifies solar compatibility and smart meter compatibility as switchboard considerations, so they should be written into the proposed scope where relevant. Clarify asbestos backing before removal

Some older ceramic fuse type boards have asbestos backing. That possibility changes the removal and disposal question, so the quotation should state whether the backing has been assessed and who is responsible for the relevant work.

The published guidance says a trained electrician can handle a small, sound, non friable board within strict limits. Otherwise, a licensed asbestos removalist does the work. The correct route depends on the condition and nature of the board, not simply on the fact that it is old.

Ask the electrician to explain the asbestos assumption before accepting the quote. Useful questions include whether removal is included, what happens if the board cannot be handled within the stated limits, whether another contractor is needed, and how the replacement will be completed after the old board is removed. Keeping that scope in writing reduces the chance of comparing one quote that includes removal with another that does not.

Compare quotes by scope, not headline price

The published range for a single phase upgrade is typically $800 to $3,500 or more. A three phase upgrade is typically $3,000 to $5,000 or more. These figures are cost signals rather than a fixed offer. The final quote comes from the assessing electrician and depends on circuit count, existing wiring condition, scope and complexity.

A useful quote should make the proposed work understandable. Check whether it identifies the phase arrangement, the number and type of protective devices, treatment of old fuses, any asbestos related allowance, solar or smart meter compatibility, and the certification steps.

  • Compare the number of circuits included.
  • Check whether existing wiring condition is addressed.
  • Ask whether circuit breakers, safety switches or RCBOs are proposed.
  • Confirm how asbestos backing is treated if it is present.
  • Check what certificate and inspection steps follow completion.

A lower total is not automatically a narrower job, but the included work needs to be clear before totals can be compared fairly.

Who this guide applies to

This guide is for Melbourne homeowners reviewing an old fuse box, limited or absent RCD protection, repeated circuit trips, planned solar or smart meter work, or a property with higher electrical demand. It also helps separate a single phase board change from a more involved three phase project.

It is not a substitute for an on site assessment. The electrician needs to inspect the switchboard, wiring and circuits before confirming the design and price. The practical next step is to describe the current board, report any electrical symptoms, identify planned equipment and request a clear quotation with the assumptions written down.

After the upgrade, the electrician issues a Certificate of Electrical Safety. The guide states that main switchboard replacement is prescribed work, so an independent licensed electrical inspector checks it as set out in Energy Safe Victoria's Certificate of Electrical Safety guidelines.

  1. Describe the current board. Share whether it uses ceramic fuses, whether circuits trip, and whether safety switches appear to be installed.
  2. Arrange an assessment. A licensed electrician checks the switchboard, wiring and circuits before preparing the quotation.
  3. Compare the scope. Check the phase arrangement, protective devices, asbestos assumptions, compatibility work and certification steps.
  4. Complete and certify. The electrician completes the installation and issues a Certificate of Electrical Safety, with prescribed work independently inspected.
