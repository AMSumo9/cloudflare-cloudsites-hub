Switchboard Upgrades Melbourne Explained

Homeowners can use Switchboard Upgrades Melbourne as a starting point for understanding the available work, but the right scope comes from an assessment of the actual board. Many older Melbourne homes still have ceramic fuse-type switchboards installed in the 1980s or earlier. Rewireable fuses, a lack of safety switches and limited spare capacity can make the board a poor fit for current household use.

Useful signs to record before requesting a quote include fuses that keep blowing, circuits that trip when the heater and kettle run together, and a board without RCD protection. These details do not establish the required upgrade by themselves, but they give the electrician a clearer starting brief. A photograph of the board, the age of the property if known, and a list of planned appliances can also help frame the assessment.

  • Note whether the board uses ceramic fuse carriers.
  • Ask whether safety switches protect the relevant circuits.
  • Record any solar, smart meter or high-draw appliance plans.

Choose protection and capacity together

A modern board can replace old fuses with circuit breakers and safety switches, with room for future circuits. An RCD safety switch is designed to cut power when it detects current leaking to earth. An RCBO combines overload protection and safety switch protection in one device, so the quote should identify which arrangement is proposed for each circuit.

Single-phase work suits many homes and may involve a straightforward board swap. Three-phase power is more commonly associated with larger homes, workshops and properties with higher-draw appliances. It is more involved and should be scoped by the assessing electrician. The useful comparison is not simply the number of phases, but the existing wiring, circuit count, equipment and future capacity the property requires.

If solar or a smart meter is planned, ask the electrician to assess compatibility as part of the same quotation. That can clarify whether the proposed board has the modern circuit protection and capacity needed for the new equipment.

Understand asbestos-backed boards

Some older ceramic fuse-type switchboards have asbestos backing. Its presence changes the removal question, so a quotation should state whether the board has been assessed and who is responsible for removal and disposal. A trained electrician may handle a small, sound, non-friable board within strict limits. Otherwise, a licensed asbestos removalist is required under the applicable WorkSafe Victoria rules.

Ask for the asbestos assumption to be written into the quote rather than treating removal as an automatic part of every replacement. The homeowner should also ask what happens if the backing cannot be handled within those limits, whether additional work will be quoted separately, and how the board will be left compliant after removal.

Read the quote beyond the headline price

The published guide gives typical ranges of $800 to $3,500 or more for a single-phase upgrade and $3,000 to $5,000 or more for a three-phase upgrade. These are starting indicators, not a fixed offer. The final quote depends on circuit count, the condition of existing wiring, the selected equipment and the complexity of the installation.

Before accepting a quote, ask for the proposed phase arrangement, number and type of protective devices, treatment of old fuses, any asbestos-related allowance, solar or smart meter compatibility, and the certification steps. Comparing quotes on those inputs is more useful than comparing totals without knowing what each electrician has included.

Who this guide applies to

This guide is for Melbourne homeowners reviewing an old fuse box, missing or limited RCD protection, repeated circuit trips, planned solar or smart meter work, or a property with higher electrical demand. It is also relevant when a homeowner needs to distinguish a simple single-phase switchboard change from a more involved three-phase project.

It is not a substitute for an on-site assessment. The electrician needs to inspect the switchboard, wiring and circuits before confirming the scope. The practical next step is to provide the board details, explain the household's current problems and future plans, then request a clear, no-obligation quotation.

After the installation, the electrician issues a Certificate of Electrical Safety. Main switchboard replacement is prescribed work, so an independent licensed electrical inspector checks it as set out in Energy Safe Victoria's certificate guidance.

  1. Describe the current board. Share whether it uses ceramic fuses, whether circuits trip, and whether safety switches appear to be installed.
  2. Arrange an assessment. A licensed electrician checks the switchboard, wiring and circuits before preparing the quote.
  3. Compare the scope. Check the phase arrangement, protective devices, asbestos assumptions, compatibility work and certification steps.
  4. Complete and certify. The electrician completes the installation and issues a Certificate of Electrical Safety, with prescribed work independently inspected.
