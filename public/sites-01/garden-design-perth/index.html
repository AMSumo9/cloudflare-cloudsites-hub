Garden Design Perth Explained

The Landscaping Perth website describes Perth gardens as projects shaped by pale coastal sand, long hot and dry summers, and rostered scheme water days. Those conditions affect the brief before a plant, path or lawn is chosen. Record where the ground dries fastest, where shade falls, and whether the site is level or sloping.

Sandy ground drains quickly and holds few nutrients, so ask what soil improver and mulch are proposed, how new planting will establish, and whether the layout limits avoidable watering. Water wise or native planting and efficient reticulation may suit a homeowner seeking lower maintenance through summer.

  • Note sun, shade, slope and likely drainage patterns.
  • Identify old lawn, rubble, limestone or material needing removal.
  • Set priorities such as planting, privacy, shade, usable space or easier upkeep.

Define the space and its use

A whole garden, front yard, backyard, pool surround, courtyard and verge each create different decisions. A front garden may need paths, driveway edges, native planting or limestone walls. A backyard may combine lawn or artificial grass with decking, shade and privacy. A pool area may require planting, paving, screening and lighting, with low mess considered in the brief.

Courtyards, villa gardens and side strips need careful use of limited space. Paving, levelling and limestone retaining should be considered with new garden beds when the block needs structural work. Write down the area to change, its intended uses, preferred materials, planting style, maintenance tolerance and any work that may need staging.

Choose design first or design and build

A design first process can help when several areas, levels or materials must work together, or when the ideas are not settled. A contractor planning and building in one go may suit a clearly defined scope with straightforward construction. The choice should reflect the size of the space and how many decisions remain open.

Ask what drawings, planting choices and material decisions are included in a design stage. For a combined process, ask who confirms levels, quantities, access and changes before work starts. In both cases, require the plan to address sandy soil, machinery access, existing lawn or rubble, retaining and paving where relevant.

Check verges and coastal conditions separately

A street verge is public land, so council limits may apply to planting, mulch and reticulation. Do not transfer a private garden layout without checking the relevant requirements. Ask which materials are acceptable, how access will be maintained, and who is responsible for keeping the finished verge manageable.

The published suburb coverage runs from Balcatta and Stirling through to Hillarys and Joondalup. Location should prompt questions about coastal exposure, block size, home type, wind, available space and access, rather than replace a site assessment.

For a northern suburbs enquiry, include photographs of boundaries, driveways, side access, retaining areas and the verge where relevant. Mention tight access, buried rubble and old lawn early because each can affect preparation and construction. Make the quote checkable

An itemised written quote should show the proposed preparation and construction before work starts. Provide the suburb, job type, approximate property area, photographs, access constraints and desired result. Ask for separate allowances for preparation, removal, retaining, paving, planting, irrigation and finishing work where they apply.

Hourly rates may suit small tidy ups and planting days, while fixed prices may suit construction work. For a larger hourly project, ask how charges will be capped and how unexpected limestone or rubble will be handled. Confirm what the homeowner supplies, how changes are approved, and when finished areas can be used.

The enquiry described by Landscaping Perth asks for the suburb, job type, property area and project notes, with photographs or other details where useful. Its website says a licensed and insured local contractor can follow up. That statement does not remove the need to check scope and quote inclusions.

Who this guide applies to

This guide suits Perth homeowners planning a whole garden, front yard, backyard, pool surround, courtyard, paving project or verge treatment. It also helps with smaller planting, mulching or soil improvement work where sandy ground or difficult access affects the brief.

It is not a substitute for site specific design when levels, retaining, drainage, pool work or council requirements need detailed assessment. Use the checklist to prepare the enquiry, then ask the relevant professional to confirm design, construction and approval details.

  1. Define the space. Choose whether the work concerns the whole garden, front yard, backyard, pool surround, courtyard, paving area or verge.
  2. Inspect the site. Record slope, sun, shade, sandy soil, access, old lawn, rubble and any retaining or levelling concerns.
  3. Set priorities. Choose between lawn or artificial grass, paving or decking, and native or mixed planting while noting maintenance expectations.
  4. Request an itemised quote. Provide the suburb, property area, job type, photographs and notes, then ask how preparation, changes and unexpected ground conditions are treated.

Common questions

What usually increases the cost of a Perth garden project? The published guidance identifies soil improvement on sandy ground, retaining on sloping blocks, site access, old lawn or rubble removal and new reticulation as cost drivers. Ask for each relevant item to appear in the written quote.

Should I choose an hourly rate or a fixed price? Hourly work may suit small tidy ups and planting days, while fixed pricing may suit construction work. For larger hourly jobs, ask how charges are capped and how limestone or rubble will be handled.

What should I ask about a street verge? Ask which council limits apply to planting, mulch and reticulation in the relevant area. A verge is public land, so its treatment should be checked separately from the private garden.

Can I handle part of the work myself? The published guidance identifies planting, mulching and soil improvement as suitable parts to undertake yourself. It also notes that autumn or winter planting lets rain help new plants settle before summer.
