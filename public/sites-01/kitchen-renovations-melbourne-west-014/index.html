Choosing between makeover and rebuild

The source page distinguishes between complete kitchen rebuilds and makeovers that update doors, benchtops and splashbacks without changing the layout. That distinction matters in older inner-west weatherboards as much as in larger growth-corridor homes, because the existing shell can either be retained or opened up for a new plan.

A refresh is usually the narrower path when the kitchen works well but looks tired. A full rebuild is the better discussion when storage, circulation, appliance placement or service locations no longer suit how the household cooks. The quote should make that boundary visible, with cabinetry, benchtop material, plumbing, electrical, splashback, appliances and finishing set out as separate scope items.

Contracts, permits and paperwork

The source provider states that projects run under a written contract with the right permits and registered plumbing and electrical trades, in line with Victorian building requirements. It also states that, for domestic building work over $10,000, a written major domestic building contract is required under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995.

Before signing, check that the contract describes the scope, price, payment stages and variations process. A fixed-price quote is only useful when the fixed scope is equally clear. If an item is provisional, excluded or subject to site conditions, it should be easy to identify before demolition begins.

  • Ask what permit or compliance paperwork applies to the agreed scope.
  • Confirm who coordinates plumbing, electrical, benchtop templating and appliance installation.
  • Ask how variations are priced, approved and recorded.
  • Keep warranty and compliance documents together at handover.

Timing from drawings to handover

The source page gives a typical full renovation range of 6 to 12 weeks from contract to handover. It breaks that into cabinetry and benchtop fabrication often taking 4 to 6 weeks, followed by an on-site stage that usually takes 2 to 4 weeks for demolition, install, plumbing, electrical and finishing.

That sequence explains why design decisions need to happen early. Drawings, material choices and lighting plans are where low-cost changes can still be made. Once cabinetry is manufactured or a benchtop is templated, a late change can affect several trades rather than one line on a drawing.

Benchtops and material decisions

The source page notes that engineered stone with 1% or more crystalline silica has been banned for manufacture, supply, processing and installation across Australia since 1 July 2024. It says homeowners are now guided toward porcelain and sintered stone, natural stone, laminate, stainless steel, solid timber and compliant low-silica alternatives.

Material choice should be discussed alongside cabinetry detail, sink type, appliance layout and edge treatment, because benchtops are measured, fabricated and installed as part of a sequence. A good question for any quote is whether the benchtop allowance reflects the actual material, slab size, joins, cut-outs and installation conditions, or whether those details are still to be confirmed.

Neutral guidance from bodies such as the Housing Industry Association, Master Builders Australia and ASIC Moneysmart can help homeowners frame broader renovation questions around contracts, budgeting and builder selection, while the project quote should still carry the exact inclusions for the individual kitchen.

Who this applies to

This guide is most relevant to homeowners planning a kitchen renovation in Melbourne's west who are choosing between a cosmetic refresh, a custom cabinetry upgrade and a full rebuild. It also suits owners of period homes who want character retained while improving day-to-day function, and owners of newer homes who need better storage, benchtop space or appliance placement.

It is less useful for renters without owner approval, small repair jobs that do not involve design or installation, and projects where the only decision is a single appliance replacement. In those cases, the right next step is narrower: confirm permission, warranty responsibility or the trade needed for that one task.

  1. Define the scope. Decide whether the job is a refresh, custom cabinetry upgrade or full rebuild before comparing quotes.
  2. Book a measure-up. Use the site visit to test the layout, storage problems, appliance locations and material preferences.
  3. Review drawings. Resolve layout, lighting and material choices on paper before cabinetry is manufactured.
  4. Check the contract. For Victorian domestic building work over $10,000, confirm the written contract sets out scope, price, payment stages and variations.
  5. Plan handover records. Ask what warranty and compliance paperwork will be supplied when the kitchen is finished.
