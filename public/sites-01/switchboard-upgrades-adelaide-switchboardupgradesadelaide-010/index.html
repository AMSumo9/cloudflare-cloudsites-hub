Switchboard Upgrades Adelaide Explained

The guidance published by Switchboard Upgrades Adelaide recommends finding out why a circuit trips before buying a new board. The cause may sit in an appliance, old wiring or the board itself, so replacing equipment without testing the problem may leave the original fault unresolved.

That distinction is especially useful in older stone villas, cottages and character homes around Kent Town, Wayville and North Adelaide. A board and the wiring behind it may come from the same era, but the electrician needs to inspect both before deciding what work is appropriate.

  • Repeated tripping should be investigated rather than repeatedly reset.
  • Blown ceramic fuses point to a board that uses fuse wire and may need modern circuit protection.
  • A damaged, crowded or worn board may need replacement, subject to the condition of connected wiring.

What a modern board changes

A full replacement swaps an old fuse or breaker panel for a modern board with individual circuit protection and safety switches. Ceramic fuse wire must be replaced when it blows, while a breaker can be reset after the underlying fault has been dealt with.

Safety switches cut power quickly when current leaks, reducing the risk of a serious electric shock. They can also be installed on an older switchboard where a complete replacement is not the selected scope. The quote should identify which circuits receive protection and whether circuits will be tested and labelled.

Power is disconnected for a period while the old board is removed and the new one installed. Lights and appliances stop during that isolation, and the duration depends on the board and the wiring behind it.

Plan capacity around the next job

A board review should include electrical work you may add later. Solar, a battery, an EV charger, ducted heating and induction cooking can change the circuits or capacity required. Spare space in a new board can help accommodate future circuits, but an empty position alone does not prove that the supply can support another large appliance.

Ask the electrician to explain the available supply, spare capacity and any need for three-phase power or a mains upgrade. The answer depends on the property and the proposed equipment, so these questions belong in the site assessment rather than being assumed from the board layout.

Quote inputs that affect the scope

A useful quote describes the planned work and its price before the supply is disconnected. It should make clear whether the job involves a full board replacement, a ceramic fuse box upgrade, safety switch installation, fault finding, circuit labelling or preparation for a new appliance.

Possible asbestos behind an old board can affect how removal is planned. Old wiring, changes to the mains supply and concealed problems may also change the scope after work begins. These are reasons to ask what has been inspected, what remains uncertain and how any additional work would be handled.

For example, if a character home has ceramic fuses and the owner is also planning induction cooking, the quote needs to address both the present protection and the proposed extra load. If an EV charger or solar system is also being considered, ask whether the supply and spare space have been reviewed together. That produces a more useful scope than pricing the board in isolation.

  • Which circuit or appliance is associated with the problem?
  • Are the wiring and supply included in the inspection?
  • Will circuits be tested and labelled?
  • Does the quote include safety switches and possible asbestos planning?
  • What document will be provided when the work is complete?

When to stop and seek urgent help

A burning smell, buzzing or hot power points near the board need urgent attention. Smoke or sparks are an emergency, so call 000. Do not treat a repeated trip, blown fuse or unusual heat as proof that the board alone is responsible, because the fault may be elsewhere in the electrical system.

Electrical installation work is not a home DIY job in South Australia. Homeowners can replace light globes and plug appliances in, but replacing a switchboard falls within electrical installation work and should stay with a licensed electrician.

Once the upgrade is complete, the electrician provides a Certificate of Compliance for the work. Keep that record with the property documents and ask which parts of the installation it covers.

  1. Record the symptoms. Note which circuit trips, which fuse blows and whether any appliance was operating at the time.
  2. Describe the property. Tell the electrician about the board, wiring concerns, access and any nearby condition that could affect the job.
  3. List future loads. Mention planned solar, battery, EV charging, ducted heating or induction cooking before the quote is prepared.
  4. Compare the written scope. Check whether the quote explains the board work, protection, testing, labelling, supply changes and possible concealed issues.
  5. Keep the compliance record. Retain the Certificate of Compliance supplied after the upgrade is finished.

Common questions

Will the power go off during the work? Yes. The supply is isolated while the old board is removed and the new one installed, so lights and appliances stop for a period. The duration depends on the board and wiring.

Does repeated tripping always mean the board needs replacing? No. The fault may be in an appliance or old wiring. An electrician needs to inspect the cause before deciding between repair and replacement.

Can safety switches be added without replacing the whole board? They can be fitted to older switchboards, although the suitable scope depends on the board, circuits and wiring condition.

What should be discussed if the home has an old backing panel? Possible asbestos behind the board should be identified during planning, so removal and handling can be included in the proposed scope.

What document is supplied after completion? The electrician provides a Certificate of Compliance for the completed upgrade.
