The source page names several borrower situations, so the guide is most relevant where one of those situations matches the next decision. A first-home buyer may need to understand deposit pressure, Queensland concessions, lenders mortgage insurance and pre-approval before making an offer. Questions To Take Into A Broker Review

A useful review should turn vague uncertainty into specific checks. The borrower does not need to know every lender rule before the meeting, but they should arrive with enough detail for the broker to test options properly.

  • What deposit amount is available now, and what part of it is genuinely usable after purchase costs?
  • What repayment level still feels comfortable if rates or household costs change?
  • Which documents are missing, unclear or likely to delay assessment?
  • Does the property involve a new estate, construction contract, small lot, flood-prone area or zoning question that needs lender checking?
  • For a refinance, what are the costs, features and debt-consolidation consequences of moving lenders?

The broker should also explain how they are paid. The source page says most mortgage brokers receive lender commission and do not charge a direct fee to standard home loan customers, while any fees or clawback arrangements should be explained upfront before proceeding.

How To Read The Recommendation

A broker recommendation should be assessed as a fit decision, not a magic approval. The lender still assesses credit, income, expenses, deposit evidence, property details and any policy conditions. The borrower should understand why a lender made the shortlist, what could still change, and which assumptions the recommendation depends on.

For an investor, that may mean asking how rental income is treated and whether interest-only options are being considered. For a first-home buyer, it may mean separating grant questions from loan approval questions. For a construction borrower, it may mean confirming whether the contract and staged payment structure suit the proposed lender.

ASIC Moneysmart encourages borrowers to consider whether a loan suits their needs and to understand loan features and costs. The same discipline applies here: ask for plain-English reasons, keep copies of key assumptions, and avoid treating a borrowing range as a promise until the lender has assessed the application.

  1. Define the loan purpose. Decide whether the next step is buying, refinancing, investing, building or seeking pre-approval before comparing lenders.
  2. Gather the evidence. Prepare income, savings, debts, living costs and property details so borrowing power can be tested realistically.
  3. Check scheme questions. For grants, concessions or low-deposit pathways, confirm current official settings before relying on a figure.
  4. Compare the shortlist. Ask why each lender suits the deposit, property type, repayment comfort and timing of the application.
  5. Confirm costs and conditions. Before proceeding, ask about fees, commissions, clawback arrangements, loan features and the remaining lender assessment steps.
